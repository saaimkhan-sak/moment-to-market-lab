00THE QUESTION

What NHL moments drive the most public engagement?

Every hockey fan understands the indescribable feeling of following the buzz after incredible, season-defining moments: whether it's an overtime winner, a hat trick, a four-point night, or an incredible 3rd-period comeback. But, behind all of that, I wanted to understand, what exactly is the pattern of that momentum across the league and how much of it is harnessable?

01THE GENESIS

Initially, I wanted to look at club revenues after key in-season moments. But, there's not enough available public data to answer that question

As I became more fascinated with the business behind the NHL, I was intrigued by the question of: could the data tell us which hockey moments sell the most tickets, move the most merchandise, create the most sponsor value, or even change fan behavior?

But, the useful data to that question is unfortunately not publicly available - it's housed in proprietary ticketing systems, commerce platforms, partnership agreements, and club-specific databases. So instead, I pivoted and found the next best proxy of publicly available data - the public attention that follows those moments.

So I changed the question instead of stretching the evidence. Public data can show when people seek information and when a club enters the news conversation. It can show whether that response arrives immediately, lingers for a few days, or disappears. That is narrower than commercial value—and far more defensible.

THE PIVOT

From private revenue data to public momentum.

  • Start with moments the league’s public record can identify.
  • Follow the public attention that comes after them.
  • Compare the first 48 hours, the next two days, and the rest of the week.
  • Turn the pattern into a club-side test—not a commercial claim.
02THE WALKTHROUGH

Every result begins with the same sequence.

Nine steps from the game sheet to an honest public finding.

The technical work is important, but the logic should still make sense without statistical training. Each step below shows both.

01

BUILD THE GAME RECORD

Start with what actually happened on the ice.

NHL schedules, box scores, play-by-play, rosters, and standings establish the game, the clock, the score, the players, and the opponent. MoneyPuck adds expected-goal and game-state context. The two sources are reconciled instead of treated as interchangeable.

INPUTgame_id + event_id + event_time_utc

Every raw response keeps its URL, retrieval time, and checksum.

02

NAME THE MOMENT FIRST

Write the rule before looking at the reaction.

An overtime win is an overtime win. A hat trick requires three goals. A comeback requires a club to trail by at least two entering the third and still win. The rules are applied to the full archive; memorable examples are never selected after the attention data is visible.

EXAMPLE LOGICif goals(player, game) ≥ 3
then moment = hat_trick

A new moment type requires a written rule, a new version, a full rebuild, and a test case.

03

RESOLVE WHO AND WHEN

Keep names, relocations, and dates from bleeding into one another.

Wikidata connects each club and player to the correct Wikipedia page, including redirects and aliases. Arizona and Utah remain separate historical identities. Vegas, Seattle, and Utah enter the record only when they existed.

IDENTITY KEYclub_id + franchise_id
+ valid_from + valid_through

All event times are stored in UTC; local time is derived separately.

04

SET A FAIR STARTING POINT

Compare each moment with the club’s own previous two weeks.

A busy club should not look important merely because it is always busy. For every qualifying moment, the prior 14 days establish the normal level of public attention. Missing days stay missing; they never become zero.

PUBLIC-ATTENTION DIFFERENCE
response during the window − average of the prior 14 days
max(average of the prior 14 days, 1)

A value of +0.20 means the public signal was 20% above its own recent baseline. It does not mean 20% more people or 20% more sales.

05

WATCH THREE CLOCKS

Separate the burst from the afterglow.

The first two days answer whether the moment broke through immediately. Days two and three test whether the story kept travelling. Days four through seven show whether anything durable remained.

06

KEEP THE SIGNALS INDEPENDENT

Ask whether people went looking—and whether the wider news cycle noticed.

Daily Wikipedia page visits represent information seeking. GDELT measures a club’s share of monitored web-news coverage after exact-name matching and a manual precision audit. They are studied separately because they describe different public behaviours.

WIKIPEDIAINFORMATION SEEKING
GDELTEARNED-NEWS PRESENCE

Neither source measures sentiment, readership, or purchase intent.

07

SEPARATE THE MOMENT FROM THE SCHEDULE

Account for the fact that no two NHL games arrive in the same setting.

A league-wide partial-pooling model adjusts for home or road, opponent, standings context, day of week, month, season, and each club’s usual level. Clubs share information where samples are thin without being forced into the same answer.

REGISTERED FORMlog(attention + 1) ~ moment × window
+ home/away + opponent + standings context
+ day of week + month + season
+ club baseline + opponent baseline

Wikipedia and GDELT are fitted separately. Raw club medians are reported beside the adjusted estimates.

08

DO NOT DOUBLE-COUNT THE STORY

Overlapping moments need different treatment.

If another major moment lands within seven days, that event is removed from the clean before-and-after comparison. It stays in the daily analysis, where both moment windows can be considered together. The exclusion reason remains visible.

OVERLAP RULEif |day(A) − day(B)| ≤ 7
then isolated(A) = false

The system never pretends one event acted alone when another major story occupied the same week.

09

MAKE THE ANSWER EARN ITS LABEL

Two signals must agree before the site calls a pattern repeatable.

Each source needs enough comparable events. The adjusted result and the club’s raw history must point the same way. Their uncertainty ranges must stay on one side of no change. If any piece fails, the site says the evidence is mixed or not clear yet.

A REPEATABLE PUBLIC PATTERN REQUIRESNadjusted ≥ 10 ∧ Nclean ≥ 10
directionWikipedia = directionnews
0 ∉ every registered 95% range

Alternative 7-day and 21-day baselines are checked before a finding is released.

03FROM EVIDENCE TO ACTION

The result is not a prediction. It is a better-timed question.

If a moment repeatedly creates a public aftershock, a club can prepare a content test around that window. The public record can tell the club when to look. Only the club’s own data can say whether the response worked.

FIRST 24 HOURS

Publish while the story is alive.

Lead with the decisive sequence and the clearest explanation of why it mattered.

24–72 HOURS

Give the moment a second chapter.

Add context, personality, or a breakdown rather than repeating the highlight.

DAY 4–7

Check whether anything remained.

Compare the public trail and the club’s private measures before making the format routine.

THE HONEST PROMISE

This project does not tell a club what a moment was worth. It tells them when the public was still paying attention—and shows every step behind that answer.
